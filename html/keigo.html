Keigo 敬語

Keigo is not one thing but a set of choices about direction. Before picking a form, answer two questions: whose action is this, and who am I talking to. Respectful forms raise someone else; humble forms lower you. Aim them the wrong way and the sentence says the opposite of what you meant.

The five categories below follow 敬語の指針, the 2007 guidelines from the Japanese Council for Cultural Affairs, which split the old three-way textbook division into five. The split that matters most in practice is 謙譲語Ⅰ against 謙譲語Ⅱ: 伺う needs someone to go to, 参る does not.

Two more layers sit alongside the three you probably learned first. 丁寧語 is the politeness you already use every day with です・ます, aimed at whoever is listening regardless of what the sentence is about. 美化語 is smaller still — a prefix that dresses up a word without pointing at anyone, which is why you can say お茶 about tea you are drinking completely alone.

A rough way to think about the five: 尊敬語 and 謙譲語 answer "who is this sentence about", 丁寧語 answers "who am I speaking to", and 美化語 answers neither — it is decoration on the word itself. Most learners can produce correct 丁寧語 early and correct 尊敬語/謙譲語 much later, because the first is one system and the second is really two, aimed in opposite directions.

Categories follow the Bunka Shingikai's 敬語の指針 (2007). Where usage is contested — お伺いする, とんでもございません — the notes say so rather than picking a side. The guidelines themselves note that native speakers disagree on several of these points, so treat "contested" as a description of real usage, not a gap in your study.

Roughly thirty verbs have irregular honorific forms that have to be learned outright. Everything else takes the productive patterns on the next tab. Tags mark whether a humble form is Ⅰ (needs a target) or Ⅱ (courteous, no target).

These patterns apply to any verb that lacks a special form. Learn お〜になる and お〜する first — between them they cover most of what you will need to produce.

Real keigo arrives in fixed phrases. Learning them whole is faster than assembling them, and it is how native speakers store them too. The last cards cover 内・外 — the rule that decides whether your own boss gets raised or lowered — plus dedicated sets for hotels and formal apologies.

Most keigo errors are not gaps in vocabulary but the same handful of structural slips. Native speakers make several of these too — which is why some appear in style guides as things to unlearn.
